# Employee benefits brokers for small employers

**Default rank 47 of 50. Total 64 out of 100.** Finance and accounting. Regulated: finance. Depends on US law or US programs.

Scores are judgment scores against [RUBRIC.md](../RUBRIC.md), not market data.

## Who the buyer is

Owner of a small benefits brokerage that helps employers choose group health and related plans.

## Who the booked calls are with

Owners and HR managers at employers with roughly 10 to 100 staff.

## Why they would pay for booked calls

One employer group renews every year, so winning a new one matters. Brokers get overwhelmed in the open enrollment period and neglect prospecting the rest of the year.

## A ready offer

I book calls with employers well ahead of their renewal, so you get a chance to review the plan outside of open enrollment.

## Opening message

Replace every part in square brackets with something true you found yourself. Never send it with a detail you made up.

> [First name], I noticed your focus on [real group size or sector]. Employers tend to stay with the same broker because no one calls them months before renewal, so I arrange those early calls. Would a list of employers near you be a useful start?

## Where to find the leads

- State insurance department licence lookups
- National benefits association member lists where public
- For their customers: US Department of Labor Form 5500 public filings, local chambers and job posts for HR roles

## What to avoid

- Quoting plan prices or savings
- Handling employee health details
- Contacting employees rather than the decision maker

## Compliance note

Benefits brokers are licensed by state. Group health information can be protected, so never request or handle employee health data. You book the call, and the licensed broker handles every plan detail. This is not legal advice.
